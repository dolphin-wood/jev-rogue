Keeping them cost nothing to store and something real to have around: each one

survive one such restatement and each came back as a defect — see the part
below on `art-requests.md`, which is the same mistake made deliberately.

### Why the first delivery failed, which is why rule 1 exists

The first delivery was drawn beautifully at roughly 341 x 256 per sprite,
about 21x oversampled against the 16 px target the spec then asked for. That
is not pixel art; it is a smooth raster picture *of* pixel art, with no real
pixel grid inside it, so downsampling had nothing to preserve and produced
mush: 106,783 distinct colours and 719,631 semi-transparent pixels in one
sheet.

The second attempt authored indexed pixel art at 32 px and lost the detail
anyway. The conclusion the project has held to since: keep the **pixel-art
look of `style-reference.png` at 64 px** rather than forcing the art into 16 px
or an eight-colour indexed palette. Hard clusters and stepped edges matter;
palette indexing does not.

## Hard rules

These are checked automatically by `pnpm assets:check`, which prints pass or
fail per rule and exits non-zero on failure. Run it before delivering.

1. **Deliver at the stated pixel size and match `style-reference.png`.** It is
   fine to work at up to 4x for control, but the delivered sprite must keep the
   reference's hard, stepped pixel edge and remain readable at 100%.
2. **Keep the shipped pixel-art look.** Use flat or two-step shading and hard
   clusters. Avoid soft gradients, painted blur and dense subpixel detail; the
   reference strip is the authority when prose and appearance disagree.
3. **Soft alpha on edges only.** At most 25% of a sprite's non-transparent
   pixels may be partially transparent. An earlier delivery had about half,
   which reads washed out and blends badly into the floor.
4. **No coloured halo.** Do not cut the alpha over a coloured background; the
   rim of that background is visible in game. At most 5% of a frame's saturated
   edge pixels may sit more than 60 degrees of hue from the frame's dominant
   hue. Near-black outlines are exempt.
5. **Centre every sprite on its collision circle.** The game positions things
   by centre, not by corner. The content bounding box centre must sit within 6%
   of the frame size from the frame centre.
6. **A dark outline on every entity**, so it reads against any floor colour
   after the room's colour tint.
7. **No text inside any sprite.**
8. **Enemy bullets stay inside the magenta band**: every visible pixel within
   25 degrees of `#ff3fa4` at saturation 0.45 or above, including outlines and
   highlights. That band is excluded from the room tint, so enemy bullets look
   identical in every room.

   **Nothing else may use that band at all.** It previously said "except the
   enemy telegraph accents", and that exception was read — reasonably — as
   licence to draw a whole enemy telegraph in magenta. The result told the
   player a shot had been fired when nothing had. The band means exactly one
   thing, *a projectile is in the air*, and a telegraph is the opposite of a
   projectile: it is the moment before one exists. Telegraph accents use the
   warm range around `#ff5544`, which is close enough to read as hostile and
   far enough not to read as ammunition.

9. **Never scale a sprite on one axis.** Pixel art stretched along one
   dimension stops matching everything around it, and it stops matching
   *itself* — a blade twice as long without being any thicker is a different
   object. If a drawing needs to cover more ground than it is drawn to cover,
   that is a design problem, not a scaling problem: a weapon that must reach
   further either gets drawn longer or stops being held. Uniform scaling is
   allowed but only at whole-number factors of the delivered size; anything
   else resamples and blurs.

## Facings

Three are drawn and the fourth is a mirror:

- `s` faces the viewer, some face visible.
- `n` is seen from behind, no face.
- `w` is a clean side profile facing left.
- `e` is generated by the game as a horizontal mirror of `w`. **Do not draw it.**

Use a 3/4 top-down view, the Zelda and Isaac convention.

Entities with one facing, radially symmetric: `turret`, `boss`, and everything
under `prop_`, `deco_`, `tile_`, `vfx_`, `icon_` and `ui_`.

## Read this first: three corrections

An earlier version of this brief produced three frames in the wrong style with
the wrong character. All three failures were caused by the brief, not by the
person drawing, so here is what changed.

### 1. One prompt per sheet, with the grid spelled out

The prompts below each describe **one sheet with an explicit row and column
layout**, because one prompt produces one image. A list of 39 frame names in
prose produces one character study, which is what happened.

Every prompt states the sheet's pixel dimensions, the number of columns and
rows, and what each row and column is. Deliver one image per prompt.

### 2. Match the existing art by editing it, not by redrawing

`assets/source/style-reference.png` is a strip of shipped frames at their
native 64 px, with transparency. `assets/source/style-reference-x4.png` is the
same at 4x for looking at. Regenerate either with
`pnpm --filter @jr/harness style-reference`.

**Attach the native strip and edit it.** The prompt that produced the approved
art the first time began "Use case: precise-object-edit. Edit the attached
three-view character strip. Keep the exact approved indigo robe, ivory trim,
shadowed face, golden eyes... detailed shading and silhouette." Style continuity
came from editing a reference, not from a description of a style.

**And the detail density has to survive 64 px.** The art is drawn smoothly but
it *lives* at 64 px, where a character is about 1,300 opaque pixels, so at that
size it reads as pixel art whether or not it was drawn that way. The first
delivery to this project failed exactly here: drawn beautifully at roughly 21x
the target and resampled down to mush. A 700 px tall character for a 64 px
frame is the same mistake at 11x.

So: **draw at no more than 4x the target** — a 256 px cell for a 64 px frame —
with **hard edges and flat or two-step shading, not soft gradients**. Check the
result at 64 px, not at full size. Detail that is not legible at 64 px does not
exist and costs readability to include.

### 3. The player is a swordfighter who casts, not a mage holding a knife

The earlier brief said to keep the wizard and swap the staff for a sword. That
was wrong, and the result reads as a mage with a dagger.

The character does cast — the design gives them a free sword swing plus three
spells — so a plain knight would misrepresent them. What the delivery got wrong
is the **silhouette proportions**, and these are the rules:

- **The blade is at least 40% of the body's height.** In the last delivery it
  was about a third and read as a dagger. The game's swing sweeps nearly two
  tiles; the weapon has to look capable of it.
- **Legs are visible from the knee down.** The floor-length shapeless robe is
  a caster silhouette. Replace it with a **short split tabard or a coat cut to
  mid-thigh** that can trail behind a dash.
- **There is a waist.** A defined torso and belt, so the figure reads as a body
  that turns rather than a cone.
- **A fully shadowed face is approved.** An earlier version of this brief asked
  for a partly visible face; that is withdrawn. The black void inside the hood
  with glowing eyes is the established look and it reads well.
- **The idle stance is a fighter's**: weight forward, front foot turned out,
  blade held up or out rather than hanging at the side.
- **The magic identity moves to accents**: the crystal in the pommel, a rune on
  the tabard, and a flame held in the off-hand. Keep the indigo and ivory, keep
  the cyan.
- **But draw the off-hand empty**, palm up with the fingers curled as if
  holding something, in **every** body sheet. The flame is a separate sprite
  (sheet 5) for the reason below.

The test: at 64 px, in silhouette alone, this must read as someone who leads
with a blade.

## Prompts

Prepend this to every prompt below.

> Draw a single sprite, top-down 3/4 view, for a dungeon action roguelike,
> delivered at exactly the stated size in the native pixel-art look of the
> attached `style-reference.png`. Use hard stepped edges and flat or two-step
> shading; keep any partial transparency on the edges only, never across the
> body, and never cut the alpha over a coloured background because the halo it
> leaves is visible in game. Sit the colours near this palette so the dungeon
> reads as one place: outlines `#0d0b1f`, dark fill `#2b2d54`, mid fill
> `#4a5480`, light fill `#8792b5`, highlight `#e8e3d8`, player accent
> `#3fa9f5`, gold `#f5a623`. Give the subject a dark outline so it separates
> from any floor colour after the room tint. Centre the subject in the frame
> with a small transparent margin on every side, and make sure it reads as a
> silhouette at 100%. Background fully transparent. No text anywhere.

### Sheet 5 — the off-hand flame, 4 frames

**Why this is separate rather than drawn into the idle frames.** The first
delivery baked a cyan flame into the off-hand of every idle frame, which looks
right in a still image and cannot work in motion: **breathing and fire want
different frame rates.** A four-frame breathing cycle runs at about a quarter
of a second per frame, and a flame at that rate reads as a slow pulse rather
than as fire, which needs something nearer 100 ms. Running the body at the
flame's rate instead makes the character pant.

Splitting it also buys something the baked version can never do. **The off-hand
becomes the readout for the resource the whole design runs on.** The player's
sword generates the mana their spells cost, so the flame can flicker while
there is mana, flare on a cast, and **go dark when mana is empty** — which is
the one thing the player most needs to know and currently has to read off a
number in the HUD. Baked into the body it is decoration; separate, it is
feedback.

> Deliver **1024 x 256, four equal columns of 256 x 256**, one row,
> alpha-transparent, no labels. 64 px target.
>
> **One orientation only — no three-view.** The camera never rotates; a facing
> is the character turning, not the view moving, so anything radially
> symmetric is seen from the same angle in every facing. That is the same rule
> that gives the turret and the boss a single facing. A flame seen from behind
> is still a flame.
>
> A small cyan flame or floating ember, matching the cyan already used for the
> pommel crystal, about the size of a fist relative to the character. Four
> frames of a **fast flicker loop**: the shape should visibly change between
> every pair of adjacent columns, not drift. Draw it as a complete object with
> its own dark outline, since it sits over the character's hand and must read
> against both the indigo robe and the floor.
>
> Do not draw a hand or any part of the character. Keep it **out of the magenta
> band** around `#ff3fa4`.

What the game does with it, so the art can be drawn against the right
expectations:

| Facing | Offset from the character's centre | Draw order |
|---|---|---|
| `s`, toward the viewer | right and slightly down | in front of the body |
| `n`, away | left and slightly up | **behind** the body |
| `w`, side profile | forward and slightly down | in front of the body |
| `e` | mirrored from `w`, which flips the offset too | in front of the body |

Two consequences for the drawing. In the back view the flame is **partly
occluded by the shoulder**, so its silhouette has to read when a third of it is
clipped — avoid a shape whose identity depends on its outer edge. And since it
carries its own dark outline, it will have that outline against the robe in the
views where it sits in front; that is accepted rather than a defect, and it is
why the outline should be thin.

### Sheet 6 — the swing strip, 1 frame, 256 x 64

**Read the first line twice: this is a straight rectangle, not a crescent.**

The crescent the player sees is this strip bent around an arc by the renderer.
If it is drawn curved, the renderer curves it again and the result is a double
spiral. Draw a straight horizontal bar.

The earlier version of this sheet asked for six hand-drawn crescents and they
could not work: measured, they spanned 146 to 215 degrees against an 80 degree
hitbox, and even corrected they would have been fixed images in a design where
reach, arc width and colour are all things upgrades change. One strip mapped
onto procedural geometry serves every combination, including a spin of several
full turns, which repeats the strip rather than needing its own art.

The same strip supports any authored visible arc from a narrow slash through
210 degrees without new artwork. Zero degrees is the mathematical degenerate
case and draws no visible crescent; use roughly 10 to 15 degrees as the minimum
when the attack still needs a readable trail. Sweeps above 140 degrees are
split into texture segments by the renderer, so 210 degrees uses two joins
instead of stretching one copy.

> Deliver **one image, 256 x 64, a straight horizontal strip**,
> alpha-transparent background, no labels, no border. Pixel art, hard edges,
> matching the shipped sheet's style.
>
> **What the two axes mean.** The long axis runs *along* the sweep: the
> renderer lays this strip end to end around the arc. The short axis is the
> **radial cross-section** of the blade trail, from one edge through the core
> to the other edge.
>
> **Uniform along its length, and seamlessly tileable left to right.** A spin
> repeats the strip several times around the circle, so the left edge must join
> the right edge invisibly. Put no brightness gradient along the length: **the
> fade from the bright leading edge to the faint tail is applied by the
> renderer per point**, and a gradient drawn in as well would be applied twice.
> Texture along the length is wanted — streaks and a little sparkle that read
> as motion — but it must repeat.
>
> **Make the cross-section symmetric top to bottom.** A bright core along the
> middle, darkening toward both long edges, with the dark outline on both. This
> is not a stylistic preference: alternate strikes sweep the other way, which
> flips the renderer's idea of which side faces outward, and an asymmetric
> cross-section would turn inside out on every second swing.
>
> **Fill the frame's height.** The renderer maps the **full texture height**
> onto the crescent's radial depth, so vertical margin is not margin — it is
> transparent padding inside the crescent. The first delivery's band covered
> 20% of its frame height, which would have produced a crescent four fifths
> empty. Leave a pixel or two at most.
>
> **One colour only: the pale blue-white**, core near `#3fa9f5` fading to
> `#e8e3d8`. The heavier finisher and any elemental affix are produced by
> tinting this at runtime, so a second colour variant would be wasted work.
> Keep it **out of the magenta band** around `#ff3fa4`.
>
> Check it with `pnpm assets:swing-check` for the production source, or
> `pnpm --filter @jr/harness check-swing-strip <absolute-strip.png>` for a
> candidate elsewhere,
> which verifies all four properties above. None of them is visible by looking
> at the strip, which is why the check exists.

**The strip does not taper, and it must not.** A tapered strip would pinch at
every seam when a spin repeats it. The taper belongs to a separate cap:

### Sheet 7 — the swing's leading cap, 1 frame, 64 x 64

> Deliver **one image, 64 x 64**, alpha-transparent, no labels. Pixel art,
> same palette and outline weight as the strip.
>
> The tapered tip of the trail. **Its left edge is the strip's cross-section**

### Sheet 9 — the lightning bolt, 3 frames, 64 x 128

An enemy attack that marks a spot on the ground and strikes it 900 ms later.

**Only the bolt is art. The ground marker is drawn by the game**, because a
marker's entire job is to state exactly which ground will be hit, its radius is
a parameter an affix can change, and it animates on the countdown. A
fixed-size sprite would misstate the area the moment the radius moved, which is
the same defect as a crescent wider than its hitbox.

> Deliver **192 x 128, three equal columns of 64 x 128**, alpha-transparent,
> no labels. **Twice as tall as it is wide**, and **anchored at the bottom
> centre**: that point is where the bolt lands, and the frame extends upward to
> show it descending.
>
> The bolt strikes from above, which is how it ignores cover, so draw the
> descent: narrow and forking near the top of the frame, thickening as it comes
> down, ending in a bright flash at the bottom centre.
>
> Column 1: the leading tip, most of the bolt not yet arrived.
> Column 2: the full bolt at its brightest, with the ground flash at its
> widest.
> Column 3: the afterimage, thinning and breaking up, the flash gone.
>
> **It must not be mistakable for a floor crack.** The `deco_crack_*` decals
> are branching lines of a similar shape and were mistaken for lightning
> during review. Three things separate them and all three are required here:

## Delivering

```
assets/sprites.png     one sheet, frames on a strict grid, no padding
assets/sprites.json    { "frames": { "<name>": { "x": n, "y": n, "w": n, "h": n } } }
```

Then:

```sh
pnpm assets:check      # validates against every rule above, exits non-zero on failure
```

Two extra checks for specific sheets, both of which have already caught a real
defect:

```sh
pnpm --filter @jr/harness style-reference             # regenerates the reference to edit from
pnpm assets:swing-check  # the four invisible strip properties on the production source
pnpm --filter @jr/harness check-arc-art <sheet.png>      # kept for any future swept-arc art
```

The checker reports violations grouped by rule: completeness in both
directions, frame size, sheet bounds, soft-alpha share, coloured fringe,
transparent margin, centring, empty frames, and bullet-family hue separation.
It is itself covered by tests that assert it accepts a valid sheet and rejects
one deliberately broken example of each rule, so a pass means something.

## What the checker cannot see

Acceptance criteria for a human reviewer:

- The player holds the sword in the same anatomical hand across all three
  facings, and **the sword stays visible in the `n` back view**.
- A dormant enemy differs from its idle **by silhouette**, not only by tint.
- A windup pose differs from idle **by silhouette**.
- The straight swing strip tiles cleanly when the renderer bends and mirrors it
  through all four attack rotations.
- Every wall case tiles seamlessly against every other.
- The companion is not mistakable for an enemy.
- The three destructible kinds differ in silhouette.
- The three reward kinds are distinguishable from across the room.
- A dropped coin or heart cannot be mistaken for a reward pickup.
- The orbiter keeps exactly four unbranched tentacles in every facing and pose.

# Revisions after playing the delivery

Everything below was found by playing the build or by measuring the delivered
frames. **Each item carries the number it was measured at**, so nothing here
needs re-deriving.

The delivery itself passed: 303 frames, nothing missing, nothing extra,
`assets:check` clean, and the decal luminance rule that `deco_stain_1` failed
in review now passes at 17 darker than the floor.

## Art to change

### 1. The off-hand flame is drawn at body scale

Measured: `vfx_offhand_0` content is **34 x 56 px inside a 64 px frame**, which
is 88% of the frame height — the same as the player's own body at 56 px. The
brief asked for "about the size of a fist relative to the character" and the
result renders as a second character standing beside the first.

**Target: about 20 px tall in the 64 px frame**, roughly a third of the body's
height. The game currently scales it by 20/56 at draw time, which works but
loses the crispness the rest of the sheet has, because a 56 px drawing shown at
20 px is a downsample.

Applies to all four frames.

### 2. The player needs a third swing pose

There are two: `windup` and `follow`. The swing runs 16 frames at 60 Hz, of
which the windup holds 4 and the follow holds 12 — so **three quarters of
every swing is a single static image**, and the first thing a player says is
that the sword does not move. For comparison the walk has four frames.

Asking for two was a deliberate call and it was wrong: the poses were cut on
the assumption that a separate crescent sprite would carry the motion, and the
crescent is now drawn procedurally as a thin trail that cannot.

**Wanted: `player_{s,n,w}_mid`**, one frame per facing, between the windup and
the follow-through — the blade at the midpoint of its travel, shoulders square,
weight transferring. The game currently fakes the motion by rotating the whole
sprite through the sweep, which reads as the body following through rather than
as a blade travelling.

### 3. The off-hand has no anchor the game can read

The flame is positioned by a table of offsets per facing, and the hand it is
meant to sit in moves in every pose. The game now adds a per-pose nudge, but
those are a plausible motion rather than a measurement: **the hand's position
in each drawn pose is not recoverable from the art.**

Two ways to fix it, either is fine:

- **A marker pixel.** Put one pixel of a reserved colour at the centre of the
  off-hand in every player frame, and the pipeline can read it out and strip
  it. This is the cheaper option and it also solves the same problem for any
  future held object.
- **Or a documented offset table**, hand-measured, 3 facings by 10 poses.

## Art that is no longer needed

- **`vfx_swing_strip`** (256 x 64) and **`vfx_swing_tip`** (64 x 64). The swing
  trail is drawn procedurally from the hitbox, which is what keeps the visual
  from ever exceeding the sector that connects, makes every reach and colour
  variant free, and is the only way a spin of several turns can be drawn at
  all. The strip was a good delivery and it passed all four of its checks; it
  simply has no consumer. **Do not redraw the crescent frames** either — the
  earlier set measured 146 to 215 degrees against the hitbox and that failure
  mode is now structurally impossible.

## Not a defect, verified

Recorded so it is not "fixed" later.

- **The walk cycle loops cleanly.** Frame-to-frame differences are 9.4, 12.7,
  8.8 and 12.7 for the south facing, and the 3-to-0 seam is in the same range
  as the others. The twitch reported in play was the game advancing frames at
  18 per second; it is now 12, measured, at 20 px of travel per frame.
- **The impact burst is correctly radial**, at 336 to 354 degrees. The angular
  limit applies only to swept arcs.

## Proposed restructure: the sword as its own layer

**This is the largest and most valuable change on the list, and it reduces the
art ask rather than adding to it.**

The sword is currently painted into the body sprite. Separating it into its
own layer, placed and rotated by the game, solves four things at once, three of
which are currently held together by workarounds.

**1. Reach becomes visible.** The drawn sword is a fixed length while
`bladeReach` is a number the stat and affix systems are meant to change, so a
reach upgrade cannot show on the character at all. This was the original
objection — a sword that covers more ground without getting longer reads as
wrong — and the present answer is a workaround: the crescent spreads further
outward while the steel stays put. A separate sword layer fixes the thing
itself.

**2. The swing animates without more drawn poses.** The sword layer can rotate
through the sweep on its own, so the body holds one pose and the blade travels.
That is the honest answer to "the sword does not move during a swing", and it
**removes the request for a third swing pose** above. The game currently
rotates the entire sprite through the sweep, which reads as the body following
through rather than as a blade moving.

**3. The mirrored facing can be corrected.** The east facing is the west
drawing flipped, and mirroring swaps which hand holds the sword — which the
spec's own acceptance criteria forbid. With the sword placed by code it can be
put on the correct side for that facing instead.

**4. Weapon variety becomes free.** A different sword is a different small
sprite on the same body, rather than a new set of body frames.

### What it needs

- **The body drawn without a sword**, with the sword hand open or gripping
  nothing, in every pose. This replaces those frames rather than adding to
  them.
- **A side-profile blade**, drawn once, that reads correctly at any rotation.
  Probably two or three at different lengths is still cheaper than what it
  replaces.
- **A grip anchor the game can read**, which is the same requirement as the
  off-hand flame's anchor above and takes the same solution: one pixel of a
  reserved colour at the grip in every pose, stripped by the pipeline. **One
  convention covers both hands.**

### What it makes unnecessary

- The third swing pose requested above.
- The whole-sprite rotation the game does during a swing.
- The per-pose offset table for the off-hand, if the same anchor convention is
  used for both hands.

# Revisions after removing contact damage

Contact damage is gone. An enemy no longer hurts the player by touching them;
it hurts them with an attack that has a facing and a window. That changed what
the art has to carry, because an attack the player is expected to dodge has to
be **visible for the whole time dodging works**, and a collision never needed
to be visible at all.

## Art to change

### 5. Enemy attacks have no drawn weapon

The rusher's thrust and the tank's slash are currently drawn as **procedural
wedges**: a filled sector for the live blade and a translucent one on the
ground for the telegraph. That is legible and it is not art. It reads as a
debug overlay, which is exactly what it is.

Two things would replace it, and the second is the cheaper one:

- **A blade or claw sprite per melee archetype**, side profile, rotatable —
  the same shape of request as the player's sword layer below, and answered by
  the same convention. The enemy's `windup` and `lunge` poses already exist; a
  separate weapon layer is what lets the weapon travel through the sweep while
  the body holds its drawn pose.
- **A ground decal for the threatened area.** The telegraph is a shape on the
  floor and it needs an edge the player can read, because the decision it asks
  for is *is one step backwards enough*. Code currently strokes an arc for
  this. A drawn sector decal — one wedge, stretched to the arc — would look
  deliberate instead of generated, and it is one frame.

The reach and arc differ per archetype (thrust: 34° blade, 1.1 tiles; slash:
40° blade sweeping 60°, 1.2 tiles), so the decal has to stretch rather than be
drawn per enemy.

### 6. There is no death pose

A body that dies is held for 200 ms as an expanding white silhouette of its
idle frame. It was drawn from a fixed south-facing frame whatever way the body
had been looking, which is fixed in code — it now uses the facing it died on —
and is recorded here only so the frame choice is not mistaken for a request.

What is missing is the pose. One drawn `death` frame per archetype,
non-directional, would carry the moment better than a stretched idle frame
does, and the white flash could then sit *under* a real pose the same way the
hit flash now sits under the tank's hit pose. Six frames.

This matters more than it sounds, because the killing blow is the one hit with
no other feedback. A surviving enemy flashes white for 110 ms over a changed
pose; a dying one is removed from the simulation on the same step it drops, so
the pop is all there is. A rusher dies in two swings, which means **half of all
hits on one are the killing blow** — and until this session the pop asked for a
frame name that did not exist (`enemy_rusher_idle0`, where the sheet names them
`enemy_rusher_s_idle0`), so every directional body died showing nothing at all.

### 7. The four ground hazards are one mechanic with four skins

`spike_strip`, `poison_pool`, `ice_patch` and `crumble_floor` are drawn
distinctly and behave **identically**: contact costs a heart, and standing
there costs another every 1.1 s. Nothing slides on the ice, nothing stacks in
the poison, and the crumbling floor does not crumble.

This is not an art defect — the art promises four things and the simulation
delivers one. It is recorded here because the art is what makes the promise,
and until the mechanics differ the distinct sprites are actively misleading:
a player who learns that ice is slippery elsewhere will read this ice wrongly.
Either the mechanics arrive or three of the four sprites are the wrong art.

Combined, ground hazards were measured at **50% of every heart lost** across
twenty-four runs, which is the other reason they need to become four different
problems rather than one problem four times.

### 8. The spell slots need icons

Three spells are bound to keys 1, 2 and 3, and the HUD currently shows each as
a **coloured pip**: lit when the key will do something, dim when it will not,
near-black when the slot is empty. That answers *can I press this* and nothing
else. It cannot answer *what happens if I do*, which is the question that
matters when the player is choosing between three of them under pressure.

An icon per castable spell is what fixes it. There are **20 castable items** in
the pool — 12 attacks, 5 payloads, 3 multicasts — and the non-castable 20
(boosts and passives) become affixes, which need their own treatment later and
are not requested here.

- **20 icons, 16 px, square**, legible at 1× against the HUD's dark strip
  (`#0d0b1f`).
- They have to read as *kinds* at a glance rather than as individual objects: a
  player learns "the red spiky one is the fire nuke" from the shape long before
  they read the name. Grouping by element and by delivery is more useful than
  20 unrelated drawings.
- **A greyed state is not needed.** The renderer tints them for cooldown and
  unaffordability, the same way the pips are tinted now.

Until they exist the HUD shows the spell's id as text, which is legible and
ugly and is the right placeholder because it is obviously a placeholder.

### 9. The orbiter has no walk cycle, and it never stops moving

Every archetype has `walk0..3` except the **turret**, which is stationary and
does not need one, and the **orbiter**, which circles the player at a fixed
radius and is therefore the one body that is *always* walking. Measured over
six seconds of play it was in motion for 314 frames out of 360 and drew a
single static frame for all of them, because the only poses it has are
`dormant`, `idle0`, `idle1` and `tele`.

The renderer now falls back to alternating its two idle frames while it moves,
which is two frames instead of one and is not a gait. It needs the same four
frames per facing the others have.

> **12 frames** — `enemy_orbiter_{n,s,w}_walk0..3`, matching sheet 20's
> existing orbiter rows in silhouette and palette.

While the sheet is open, two smaller gaps in the same class:

- **No `windup` or `lunge` for the tank's ram.** It has both, drawn for the
  sweep it used to do, and the attack is now a charge — a body throwing its
  shoulder forward rather than swinging. The existing frames read as a wind-up
  and a lunge well enough to ship, so this is a want rather than a need.
- **No struck pose except the tank's.** `hit0/hit1` exist for the tank alone.
  Every other body carries a hit on a white flash over whatever pose it was
  already in, and the flash is doing work the art should: a hit now
  *interrupts* what an enemy was doing, and a body that visibly reels sells
  that far better than a tint does.
>
> **30 frames** — `enemy_{rusher,shooter,orbiter,summoner,turret}_*_hit0..1`,
> at the same facings each archetype already uses (the turret has none).

### 10. The dormant pose is a single frame, and it is the pose seen most

`dormant` is one drawing per facing, and it is what a body shows for the whole
time before it notices the player — which, in a room the player is picking
their way through, is most of the time they spend looking at it.

One frame cannot animate, and the two attempts to work around that both
failed in instructive ways:

- Making unaware bodies **drift** around their spawn produced a jitter: the
  drift force was small enough that its direction flipped every few frames,
  so the body vibrated in place and its facing spun to follow.
- Alternating `dormant` with `idle0` produced a **sway**: they are not two
  frames of one motion, they are two different poses, so the body stood up and
  sat down once a second. On the tank it read as rocking.

What ships instead is a single drawing with a slow breathing scale applied by
the renderer, plus a slow patrol for the light archetypes. It is fine. It is
not as good as a drawn pair.

> **15 frames** — `enemy_{rusher,shooter,orbiter,summoner,tank}_{n,s,w}_dormant1`,
> a second frame of the *same* pose: the same silhouette with a breath of
> movement in it, so the pair reads as one settled body rather than as two
> states. The turret needs one too (`enemy_turret_dormant1`), for 16 in total.

### 11. `vfx_impact_0..2` are directional, and a hit is not

The three impact frames read as **crescents with a direction in them**. That is
a good slash decal and the wrong shape for the mark of a hit, which has to look
the same whichever side the blow came from — used omnidirectionally they made
every strike look like a small sword swipe pointing somewhere arbitrary, and
spraying one per particle looked like confetti.

What ships is a drawn expanding ring, which has no direction to be wrong about
and can be scaled by how much the hit mattered without a second drawing.

Two ways to make the delivered frames usable, either is fine:

> **Re-drawn omnidirectional**, 3 frames: a radial burst — spikes or a ring of
> shards thrown outward from the centre — so it can be stamped at any hit with
> no rotation. This is the version the renderer wants.
>
> **Or kept as-is and repurposed**: they work as a *slash* mark, oriented along
> the arc, for the player's own sword connecting. That needs no new art at all,
> only a decision that these belong to the sword rather than to every hit.

Also unused and worth a decision: `vfx_swing_strip` and `vfx_swing_tip` were
superseded by the procedural crescent and remain the only genuinely dead art in
the sheet.

### 12. The shooter's side view faces the wrong way

The sheet's convention is that the `_w_` drawing is a side view facing **west**,
and the east facing is that drawing mirrored. Measured across the roster by the
position of each sprite's accent pixels — eyes, lights and gold trim, which is
where a body's front is — four of the five side views follow it:

| Frame | Accent position | Faces |
|---|---|---|
| `enemy_rusher_w_idle0` | 0.36 | west |
| `enemy_tank_w_idle0` | 0.24 | west |
| `enemy_summoner_w_idle0` | 0.18 | west |
| `enemy_orbiter_w_idle0` | 0.24 (warm) | west |
| `enemy_shooter_w_idle0` | **0.57 (its barrel)** | **east** |

The shooter's barrel points right. Mirrored for the east facing it therefore
aims *away* from the player, which is half the compass — and that is the

> **Redraw `enemy_shooter_{w}_*` mirrored**, so its barrel points west like
> every other side view, and the exception can be deleted. 8 frames
> (`dormant`, `idle0`, `idle1`, `tele`, `walk0..3`).

`enemy_tank_*` is worth a second look for a related reason: it is drawn almost

### 13. The dropped heart is blue; the HUD heart is red

`pickup_heart_0/1` has a body colour of `26,26,246` — pure blue — while

unknown item rather than as health.

The renderer tints the drop to the HUD's red, which works because the outline
is dark and a multiply leaves it dark. It is a workaround.

- `pickup_coin_0/1` is correct at `252,183,60`.

### 14. There is no ground-fire art, and no lightning-strike art either

The thrown-flame attack leaves **burning ground** and the lightning strike
leaves a **marker then a bolt**, and both are drawn procedurally because
nothing in the sheet covers them. `prop_brazier_0/1` is a fire but it is a
standing object on a plinth, not a patch on the floor.

The procedural fire has been rewritten once already, and the reason is worth
recording: the first version was two filled translucent circles, which reads
as a **stain** rather than as a fire — a player could not tell it was
dangerous, which for the one hazard that removes floor from play is the entire
job. What works without art is a dark charred base (scorched stone is *darker*
than stone; a bright wash over it only lowers contrast) with **tongues rather
than area** standing on it: tapered spikes of varying height, flickering on
each patch's own clock. Legible, and obviously generated.

> **Ground fire, 4 frames**, 64 px: a patch of flame on stone seen from the
> game's angle, looping. Dark scorch at the base, ragged vertical silhouette,
> and the loop readable at 8 fps. It has to work at any size, since the radius
> is a parameter, so a tileable or centre-scalable patch is better than a
> fixed footprint.
>
> **Lightning strike, 3 frames** for the bolt already exist (`vfx_bolt_0..2`)
> and are wired. What is missing is the **marker**: the ring on the floor that
> counts down before the bolt lands. Code draws a stroked circle closing
> inward. 2 frames, 64 px, would let it read as a rune or a charge rather than
> as UI.

Both are wants rather than needs — the procedural versions are legible now.
They are the last two effects in the game with no drawn art at all.

### 15. The summoner's side view faces the wrong way

Same class as the shooter in note 12, and the opposite conclusion — recorded
together because the pair is instructive about how to decide this.

The summoner's `_w_` drawing puts its hood, its eye and its held orb all on
the **same side**, so no pixel measurement can separate a face from a trailing
hood. Three attempts disagreed: whole-sprite accent centroid said one thing,
warm-only said another, and a hood-band cyan measurement caught the orb
instead of the eye. What settled it was play — in motion, with a known player
position, it faces away — and play is the ground truth for a facing.

The renderer carries a one-entry exception for it, which works and is a lie in
the data.

> **Redraw `enemy_summoner_w_*` mirrored**, so its hood and orb point west like
> every other side view, and the exception can be deleted. 8 frames
> (`dormant`, `idle0`, `idle1`, `tele`, `walk0..3`).

**The general rule for the next one**: a side view should put the face on the
side it travels and let the trailing features fall behind it, and if that is
ambiguous in the drawing it will be ambiguous in code. `pnpm assets:facing-check`
renders every archetype at every facing with a marker on the side the body
should be looking at — check a new body against that before it ships, not after.

## The perspective, and the one rule it imposes

The floor is drawn looking **straight down** and the bodies are drawn in
**three-quarter view** — you see their faces while you see the top of the
paving. That mismatch is deliberate and it is the standard convention: Zelda,
The Binding of Isaac and Enter the Gungeon all do exactly this, because a true
top-down creature is an unreadable blob and a true side-on floor cannot be
walked around in two axes.

What makes it hold together is **how shadows are handled**, and there is one
rule:

> **A body's shadow sits directly beneath it and is never offset.**

And, for anything drawn as its own frame:

> **A shadow is drawn on the body's foot line, not in the middle of its frame.**

Every delivered `shadow_*` frame centres its blob vertically in the frame,
while the bodies' feet sit at 84% to 94% of frame height. Drawn at matching
centres the shadow lands at the **waist**, which is what shipped and what was
reported. The renderer now measures both — the body's lowest opaque pixel and
the shadow blob's own centre — and lines those up instead, so the placement
survives a redelivery; but a shadow drawn where the feet are needs no
correction at all.

A shadow is also **not wider than the body casting it**. `shadow_player` spans
55 of its 64 px while the player spans 50, and at native scale the blob read as
a stain on the floor rather than as a shadow, so the renderer narrows it.

An offset shadow implies a light source and a surface *behind* the subject,
which is side-view language. Dropped down and to the right on a top-down
floor, an enemy read as a sticker pasted onto a wall — which is exactly what
was reported. A shadow directly under the feet says only *this is standing on
the ground*, which is the single thing it has to say.

The same rule is why the floor decals, the scorch marks and the hazard tiles
carry no directional lighting: the moment one of them has a highlight on one
side, the floor acquires a light direction the bodies do not share.

Two consequences for anything new:

- **Props and scenery** get a contact shadow, not a cast shadow. `prop_break_*`
  already has a small one baked in, which is correct.
- **Contrast against the floor** cannot be solved with a shadow, because the
  only shadow available is under the body. It has to come from the drawing —
  which is note 16.

### 16. The enemies are drawn at the floor's brightness

This was first written up as a turret problem. Measured, it is the whole
roster, and the measurement is worth keeping because it names the axis:

| | mean luminance | vs floor |
|---|---|---|
| **floor tiles** | **85.0** | — |
| `enemy_summoner` | 84.9 | **−0.1** |
| `enemy_rusher` | 86.1 | +1.1 |
| `enemy_tank` | 87.5 | +2.5 |
| `enemy_shooter` | 89.3 | +4.3 |
| `enemy_turret` | 90.5 | +5.5 |
| `enemy_orbiter` | 104.3 | +19.3 |
| `player` | 67.5 | −17.5 |

**Five of the six enemies sit within six points of the ground.** In value terms
they *are* the floor, and the summoner is indistinguishable from it. The two
sprites that have never been hard to pick out — the orbiter and the player —
are the two with real value contrast, one lighter and one darker.

This is why no shadow fixed it. A shadow darkens the floor; the problem is the
body. It is also why the mood transform cannot help: it moves both equally.

> **Push each enemy's value away from 85.** Either direction works and mixing
> them is better than picking one: the player is the dark silhouette, so
> enemies reading *lighter* than the ground keeps the two classes apart at a
> glance. A target of at least ±12 mean luminance is the ask; the orbiter
> already meets it and is the reference.
>
> Hue is not the lever. Value is what survives at small size, in motion, at low
> brightness and for a colour-blind player — and this game is all four.

**The test, so it does not regress**: desaturate a screenshot and check the
silhouettes still read. `pnpm assets:facing-check` renders the roster on a flat
ground for exactly this kind of inspection.

**What ships meanwhile** is a hairline dark outline behind every body, four
copies of the frame at half-world-pixel offsets. It works — the renderer runs
un-rounded and anti-aliased, so half a pixel is a genuine hairline — and it is
a compromise: a body's edge should come from its own drawing.

### 17. Shadows should be drawn, one per archetype

Three procedural shadows were tried and the first two failed for reasons that
are about *shape*, not tuning — worth recording because the next attempt will
otherwise repeat one of them:

- **A wide ellipse**, three times the body's radius, read as dirt on the floor.
  A shadow is the shape of the thing casting it, and that was not the shape of
  anything.
- **The sprite's own silhouette**, squashed and laid at the feet. Correct for a
  body with one coherent mass, and wrong for a radial one: the turret's arms
  are separated, so flattening them produced a scatter of disconnected blobs
  that read as debris.
- **A small tight ellipse** at the base, hard-edged, one flat alpha. What
  ships. It grounds the body and says nothing else.

The reference asset packs for this genre ship a **drawn shadow sprite per
character**, often with a normal and a half-strength variant, precisely because
the right shadow depends on the silhouette and no formula gets it right for
every shape.

> **One shadow frame per archetype**, 6 frames, 64 px (96 for the tank): the
> body's cast shadow on flat ground, hard-edged, a single flat dark value, no
> gradient. Drawn as one connected mass even where the body's parts are
> separated — that is the whole thing a formula cannot do.
>
> Naming: `shadow_<archetype>`, undirected. A per-facing set would be more
> accurate and is not worth six times the frames; the flattened silhouette
> barely changes between facings.
>
> The player needs one too (`shadow_player`).

**A soft gradient is the one thing to avoid.** It is the instinct from
non-pixel art and it is the wrong instinct here: every edge in this game is
hard, and a blurred shadow is the first thing that reads as "not part of the
sprite work".

## The sword now floats, and that is a design change

Requesting the sword as its own layer solved the problem it was asked to solve
and created a new one that no amount of art could fix.

The delivered blade is a normal sword: 37 art px grip to tip, about 18 world px
at native scale. The hitbox reaches a full tile, 32. A sword that visibly falls
short of where it hits feels broken to swing — the player aims by the drawing,
so every connect becomes a surprise. Two ways to close that gap were tried and
both are bad art:

- **Stretch the x axis** to 32. A long thin strip, out of proportion with the
  body holding it, and against the rule above.
- **Scale both axes.** A club as thick as the player's torso, at a non-integer
  factor that resampled the pixels into a blur.

Neither is fixable, because for a *held* weapon reach **is** length, and this
one needs to be 1.7x longer than it is drawn.

So it is not held. It is a **magic sword that hovers in front of the caster**,
and reach then comes from where it floats rather than from how long it is: the
grip sits 13.5 px out along the facing, the blade points outward, and the tip
lands exactly on the hitbox radius with the drawing at native, unstretched,
whole-pixel scale.

This is a better fit for what was already on screen. The character is a hooded
caster with a glowing orb in the off hand, not a knight — and a blade that
orbits its owner is a far more natural explanation for a 170 degree arc than a
human shoulder is. It also opens a line of content the design did not have:
**flying-blade spells**, which are now consistent with the basic attack rather
than an exception to it.

Three consequences for the art, none of which need new frames:

- The **hand anchors are no longer used for the sword**. They stay for the
  off-hand flame, which really is held.
- The blade rests along the **facing**, not at the authored `bladeAngleDeg`.
  That angle points up and to the right in every facing, so while it drove the
  float, facing *down* still put the sword above the player's head.
- The orbit is a **circle, not an ellipse**. Compressing it vertically was
  tried on the reasoning that the floor is top-down and the bodies are
  three-quarter — true, and why the shadows are ellipses — but what the float
  has to clear is the body, and the body is not wider than it is tall. Shadows
  are ellipses because they lie on the floor.

## The procedural blade is gone

`crescent.ts` drew the steel itself: a quadrilateral from the body out to the
crescent's inner edge, repeated three times along the path with falling alpha.
It existed because there was no drawn sword.

With `weapon_player_sword` on screen it became a **second sword** — a pale
generated blade sweeping alongside the real sprite at a slightly different
length and angle. It was reported three times, which is what a thing looks like
when it is easy to mistake for the trail drawn next to it.

Deleted. What remains of the crescent is the trail band and the sparks, which
are the parts that are genuinely not the weapon: they are where the weapon has
been.

defect: it is drawn almost front-on rather than in profile, so mirroring barely
changes its silhouette and it reads as facing the viewer whichever way it
walks. That is why its facing was hard to judge by eye.

# P2 — The dormant pose is one frame, and it is the pose seen most

`dormant` is a single drawing per facing, and it is what a body shows for the
whole time before it notices the player — which, in a room the player is
picking their way through, is most of the time they spend looking at it.

One frame cannot animate, and both workarounds failed instructively:

- Making unaware bodies **drift** produced a jitter — the drift force was small
  enough that its direction flipped every few frames, so the body vibrated and
  its facing spun to follow.
- Alternating `dormant` with `idle0` produced a **sway**. They are not two
  frames of one motion, they are two different *poses*, so the body stood up
  and sat down once a second. On the tank it read as rocking, and was reported
  twice.

What ships is a slow random walk with a pause at each stop, drawn from the
`idle0`/`idle1` pair. It is fine. It is not as good as a drawn dormant pair.

> **16 frames** —
> `enemy_{rusher,shooter,orbiter,summoner,tank}_{n,s,w}_dormant1` (15) plus
> `enemy_turret_dormant1` (1).
>
> A second frame of the **same** pose: the same silhouette with a breath of
> movement in it, so the pair reads as one settled body rather than as two
> states. This is the distinction the `dormant`/`idle0` experiment got wrong.

# P2 — Only the tank has a struck pose

`hit0`/`hit1` exist for the tank alone. Every other body carries a hit on a
white flash over whatever pose it was already in.

This became more important than it was: a hit now **interrupts** what an enemy
is doing — a 190 ms stagger that cancels attacks and movement, the Hades model
— and a body that visibly reels sells that far better than a tint does. The
flash is currently doing work the art should.

> **26 frames** — `enemy_{rusher,shooter,orbiter,summoner,turret}_*_hit0..1`
> at the facings each archetype already uses. The turret has no facing and
> needs 2; the other four need 6 each. The earlier total of 30 was arithmetic,
> not a hidden set of four additional names.

# P3 — There is no death pose

A body that dies is held for 200 ms as an expanding white silhouette of its
idle frame, at the facing it died on.

> **6 frames** — `enemy_<archetype>_death`, non-directional, one per archetype.

The white flash could then sit *under* a real pose, the same way the hit flash
now sits under the tank's hit pose.

This matters more than it sounds, because **the killing blow is the one hit
with no other feedback.** A surviving enemy flashes white for 110 ms over a
changed pose; a dying one is removed from the simulation on the step it drops,
so the pop is all there is. A rusher dies in two swings, which means half of
all hits on one are the killing blow.

# P3 — The spell slots need icons

Three spells are bound to keys U, I and O, and the HUD shows each as a
**coloured pip** — lit when the key will do something, dim when it will not,
near-black when the slot is empty. That answers *can I press this* and nothing
else. It cannot answer *what happens if I do*, which is the question that
matters when choosing between three under pressure.

Any of 18 castable items can land in a slot, so all 18 need one. The other 22
items in the pool are boosts and passives that become affixes; they need their
own treatment later and are **not** requested here.

**One more since the earlier delivery: `icon_shock_arc`**, the second starting
spell — a short spark that chains to two more bodies. Before this pass the
card and HUD fell back to the spell reward's kind art. Same footing as the
other seventeen: 16 x 16, legible at 1x, a forked spark in the sheet's cool
cyan-white.

**Three more castables and one stat since then**, all on the same footing and
all falling back to kind art until drawn: `icon_spirit_blades` (three small
blades in a ring — the orbit spell), `icon_wildfire_field` (a low patch of
flame — the ground field), `icon_stone_ward` (a squat pillar — the raised
wall), and `icon_stat_wrath` (one hot-orange segment of a gauge — the rage the
spin spends).

> **21 frames, 16 x 16, square** — `icon_<id>`, legible at 1x against the
> HUD's dark strip `#0d0b1f`.

| Kind | ids |
|---|---|
| attack (13) | `magic_bolt`, `shock_arc`, `spark_spray`, `stone_shard`, `ember_dart`, `frost_needle`, `venom_spit`, `arc_lance`, `scatter_shot`, `cinder_burst`, `glacier_spike`, `void_orb`, `plague_bloom` |
| payload (5) | `impact_carrier`, `fuse_carrier`, `wall_carrier`, `piercing_carrier`, `mortar_carrier` |

They have to read as **kinds** at a glance rather than as individual objects: a

# P3 — There is no ground-fire art

The thrown-flame attack leaves **burning ground** and it is drawn procedurally,
because nothing in the sheet covers it. `prop_brazier_0/1` is a fire but it is
a standing object on a plinth, not a patch on the floor. It has been reported
as unreadable: *"is that burning? can't tell."*

The procedural version has been rewritten once and the reason is worth having:
the first was two filled translucent circles, which reads as a **stain** rather
than a fire — and for the one hazard that removes floor from play, "is this
dangerous" is the entire job. What works without art is a dark charred base
(scorched stone is *darker* than stone; a bright wash over it only lowers
contrast) with **tongues rather than area** standing on it: tapered spikes of
varying height, flickering on each patch's own clock.

> **4 frames, 64 px** — `vfx_groundfire_0..3`. A patch of flame on stone seen
> from the game's angle, looping, readable at 8 fps. Dark scorch at the base,
> ragged vertical silhouette.
>
> **The radius is a parameter**, so it has to work at any size: tileable, or
> scalable from the centre, is better than a fixed footprint.

# P3 — The lightning strike has no floor marker

The bolt frames exist and are wired (`vfx_bolt_0..2`). What is missing is the
**marker**: the ring on the floor that counts down before the bolt lands. Code
draws a stroked circle closing inward, which reads as UI rather than as magic.

The marker is load-bearing, not decoration — it is the whole reason the strike
is fair, and its duration is sized against a 250 ms human reaction time.

> **2 frames, 64 px** — `vfx_strikemark_0..1`. A rune or a charge on the floor,
> not a UI ring. The renderer scales it and fades it in over the countdown, so
> the drawing itself should not encode progress.

# P4 — The turret has one lit frame, so a live turret is a still

An awake turret now **holds** `enemy_turret_tele`, which is how the player
tells armed from dormant — it is area denial, so the question the player asks
is whether a patch of floor is currently expensive, and a turret cannot answer
by walking at them the way every other body does.

That gave it three states from two drawings: the `idle0`/`idle1` pair with its
cyan core is *powered and scanning*, `tele` with its magenta core and lit gems
is *locked on*, and a red flash over `tele` is *firing now*.

The cost is that the locked-on state does not animate. It cannot be fixed by
alternating `tele` with an idle frame, because their cores are different
*colours* — that flicks between magenta and cyan, which reads as two states
fighting rather than one motion. What ships is an additive copy of the same
frame with a pulsing alpha, so only the core and gems breathe.

> **1 frame** — `enemy_turret_tele1`, a second frame of the lit pose. Same
> silhouette, the glow at a different intensity. Then the lit state is a real
> pair and the additive hack goes away.

# P4 — Drawn shadows, one per archetype

Three procedural shadows were tried and the first two failed for reasons about
**shape**, not tuning:

- **A wide ellipse** at three times the body radius read as dirt on the floor.
  A shadow is the shape of the thing casting it, and that was not the shape of
  anything.
- **The sprite's own silhouette**, squashed and laid at the feet. Correct for a
  body with one coherent mass, wrong for a radial one: the turret's arms are
  separated, so flattening them produced a scatter of disconnected blobs that
  read as debris.
- **A small tight ellipse** at the base, hard-edged, one flat alpha. What
  ships. It grounds the body and says nothing else.

Reference asset packs in this genre ship a drawn shadow per character precisely
because the right shadow depends on the silhouette and no formula gets it right
for every shape.

> **7 frames** — `shadow_<archetype>` for the six enemies plus `shadow_player`.
> 64 px, 96 for the tank. Undirected: a per-facing set would be more accurate
> and is not worth six times the frames, since the flattened silhouette barely
> changes between facings.
>
> The body's cast shadow on flat ground. Hard-edged, a single flat dark value,
> no gradient. **Drawn as one connected mass even where the body's parts are
> separated** — that is the whole thing a formula cannot do.

**A soft gradient is the one thing to avoid.** It is the instinct from
non-pixel art and it is the wrong instinct here: every edge in this game is
hard, and a blurred shadow is the first thing that reads as "not part of the
sprite work".

# P4 — Repurpose `vfx_impact_0..2` as sword-hit marks

The three impact frames read as **crescents with a direction in them**. That is
a good slash decal and the wrong shape for the mark of a hit, which has to look
the same whichever side the blow came from. Used omnidirectionally they made
every strike look like a small sword swipe pointing somewhere arbitrary, and
spraying one per particle looked like confetti — which is what was reported.

What ships is a drawn expanding ring, which has no direction to be wrong about
and scales by how much the hit mattered without a second drawing.

The old order left two possible resolutions. This consolidated order chooses
one so the implementation is not forced to guess:

> **Keep the three existing frames unchanged and repurpose them as the
> player's sword-hit mark**, rotated to the tangent of the swing. They are not
> used for enemy projectiles, fire, lightning or generic damage. Generic hits
> keep the procedural expanding ring, whose symmetry is correct for that job.

This request adds and redraws no art; it gives the delivered directional art a
directional consumer.

# Do not draw these

Requests that were withdrawn after measurement or shipped in code. They are
listed so they are not picked up from the old document.

- **The shooter's side view is correct.** It was reported as mirrored on the
  strength of an accent-pixel measurement that put its mass right of centre —
  but the large cyan shapes on that side are its **fins**, and the small
  magenta dot on the other side is its **eye**. An eye is a face. Measuring the
  warm accents only put it at 0.34, in line with every other body. The code
  exception has been removed. *The lesson for the next one of these: find the
  eye, not the brightest mass.*
- **The four ground hazards no longer need to become four sprites.** They were
  drawn distinctly and behaved identically, which made the art a false promise.
  The mechanics now differ — poison ticks a slow, ice slides, crumbling floor
  collapses — so the distinct sprites are correct as delivered.
- **No seamless ice, poison or spike floor tile is wanted.** The three framed
  plates were stamped once per cell, so a 3 x 3 ice patch read as nine little
  windows. The renderer now composes a zone as one plate — quarter-tiles, the
  frame's interior on every shared edge, the rim only round the outside — from
  the delivered frames. A tileable variant would be redundant.
- **`vfx_swing_strip` and `vfx_swing_tip` are dead.** Superseded by the
  procedural crescent. They are the only genuinely unused frames in the sheet;
  no replacement is wanted.
- **A third painted player swing pose is not wanted.** The separate
  `weapon_player_sword` layer supplies the missing motion and keeps variable
  reach honest.
- **The tank's `windup` and `lunge` are fine.** They were drawn for a sweep and
  the attack is now a charge, but they read as a wind-up and a brace well
  enough to ship. The renderer also reuses `windup` for the skid at the end of
  a charge, which is the same shape a mass arriving makes.
- **Walk cycles do not need more frames.** Measured: a walk runs at
  `speed / stride` frames per second, which does **not** change with how many
  frames the cycle has — six frames at an 11 px stride is still 3 fps, just a
  longer loop. The tank and the summoner were running at 3.0 and 5.7 fps and
  have been fixed by flooring the stride in code, so every cycle now runs at 8
  fps or better with the weights intact (rusher 11.5, shooter 8.1, orbiter
  10.3, tank 8.0, summoner 8.0). **Four frames is the right count.** If
  movement still reads poorly it is the P1 value problem, not the frame count.

# The affix icons, 12 frames

**New request.** Doc 013 gives every spell three affix slots and doc 006 now
carries the pool: twelve affixes, three tiers each, every one of them a change
to an *event* rather than a number. They appear in three places — on a reward
card, in a spell's three slots on the equipment screen, and as a small mark
beside the spell's own icon in the HUD — so one icon has to work at two sizes.

> **12 frames, 16 x 16, square** — `icon_affix_<id>`, on the same footing as
> the twenty spell icons already delivered: legible at 1x against the HUD's
> dark strip `#0d0b1f`, read as a *kind* at a glance before the name is read.

| Frame | Affix | Fires |
|---|---|---|
| `icon_affix_fork` | Fork | on hit |
| `icon_affix_chain` | Chain | on hit |
| `icon_affix_brand` | Brand | on hit |
| `icon_affix_harvest` | Harvest | on kill |
| `icon_affix_echo` | Echo | on kill |
| `icon_affix_bloom` | Bloom | on expire |
| `icon_affix_shatter` | Shatter | on wall |
| `icon_affix_repeat` | Repeat | on cast |
| `icon_affix_scatter` | Scatter | on cast |
| `icon_affix_ward` | Ward | on cast |
| `icon_affix_retort` | Retort | on hurt |
| `icon_affix_slipstream` | Slipstream | on dash |

Three things that make this set different from the spell icons:

- **They are verbs, not objects.** A spell icon can draw the thing it throws;
  an affix has no object of its own, only a moment and a consequence. Splitting,
  arcing, marking, repeating — the shape has to say what *happens*, which
  usually means an arrow, a division or a repetition rather than a thing.
- **Group them by hook, not by element.** The player's question is "when does
  this go off", and four of the twelve fire on impact, two on a kill, three on
  the cast. A shared visual motif per group is worth more than twelve unrelated
  drawings, exactly as with the spells.
- **No tier variants.** An affix has three tiers and they use the **same**
  icon; the tier is shown by the renderer as pips beside it. Three drawings per
  affix would be 36 frames to say a number, and the number is already written.

**One frame is already covered.** `icon_affix_repeat` replaces the old
`icon_double_cast` / `icon_triple_cast` / `icon_chorus_cast`, which were drawn
for items that cannot work in a keyed spell slot (doc 013). Those three
delivered icons are now unused and can be **redrawn as affix icons** rather
than added to: they are the right subject, attached to the wrong thing.

### Two new enemy sheets

Two archetypes ship as **tinted variants** of existing sheets until they have
their own: the **lancer** on the rusher's frames (warm tint, a tenth larger)
and the **sentinel** on the turret's (green tint). Each wants the full set its
base has — walk, idle, dormant, hit, death, and the attack pose (windup and
lunge for the lancer, tele for the sentinel) at three facings where the base
has them. The lancer is a lean, long-limbed body carrying a **lance** — the
charge is the whole identity, so the weapon is most of the silhouette. The
sentinel is a squat emplacement with a **single barrel** rather than the
turret's radial lens: it aims and fires slow shots, and the barrel is what
tells the player it is not a turret.

**The lancer and the rusher changed while this was open.** Neither charges:
both walk up and drive their spikes out all round, the lancer from further
out, and the lancer's elite bursts eight spikes across the room when it
dies. So the lancer's sheet wants **spikes, not a lance** — a lean, spined
body, the rusher's shape drawn taller — a two-frame spike drive for each
body (braced with sockets closed, then sockets raised). The renderer places
eight independent `vfx_spike_*` layers on those sockets so the same parts can
extend, hold and detach. The sentinel follows the same separation rule: its
body has a pivot socket and `weapon_enemy_sentinel_barrel` rotates over it.
One body-only frame is used for the lancer burst:

> **Death burst, 1 frame at 64 x 64** — `enemy_lancer_burst`: the body
> braced open with eight exposed sockets and no full spikes baked in. The
> renderer extends and releases the eight independent spike layers.
